13:30

A 68-year-old woman is at lunch with her family. Without warning her right arm goes weak, and she cannot find her words.

An ambulance brings her in. In a stroke, the clock decides what can still be done. The clot-busting drug is for the first four and a half hours; after that the rulebook stops recommending it.

16:00

She is in the scanner, two and a half hours after it began. Her stroke score is 8, and before today she was fully independent. Two hours of the window are left.

Challenge 1

This is a plain CT, the first thing the scanner returns. Somewhere in it a stroke is under way. Tap the scan where you think it is. A real research scan (ISLES'24, case D-10) stands in for hers.

Here is what the first model marked: the core, tissue already lost, inside a wider area still at risk. The white line is where the damage finally settled. Early on, a stroke barely shows on a plain CT.

A second model reads the plain scan for bleeding, because a bleed rules the drug out.

Our patient is scripted, so her numbers are written by hand in the format the two models produce: a tiny core, a small area at risk, a clot in a branch artery called M2, and no bleed. These numbers are all the language model will ever see of a scan.

The rulebook

The guideline cannot be copied, so the team rewrote every criterion in its own words: 47 passages, each pointing to its source. Tap one to read it.

A program applies them first, with no language model involved. Run it yourself: tap each of her facts to feed it to the rule for the clot-busting drug.

Now clot removal. Feed the facts again. One piece is missing: whether her M2 branch is the dominant one, which this system cannot see. The rulebook answers undetermined instead of guessing.

What will the language model recommend?

Challenge 2

Now the language model, Claude Sonnet 5.5, gets the same file: the scan's numbers, the triage note and all 47 passages. Before it answers, make your guess.

Its answer: give the drug; do not attempt removal. On the drug it matches the rulebook. On removal the rulebook was undetermined, so either answer is accepted.

Five other models read the same file. All six would give the drug. One small model would also attempt the removal.

05

The marking

A right answer is not the end. You mark it first.

ClaimsChallenge 3 of 3

Tap every claim you would not trust.

    Challenge 3

    Everything the model wrote is split into single claims, eight of them here. You are the verifier. Tap any claim you would not trust.

    The real verifier checks each one: numbers against the scan, patient facts against the note, and every cited passage against the 47 in the rulebook.

    Two fail. To justify the drug, Sonnet cited a passage that does not exist. The rulebook has a passage with a similar name. The model wrote one that is not in the book.

    The right decision, resting on an invented source. That is what this project measures: not only whether a model is right, but whether its working can be trusted.

    The bigger picture

    The same test ran on 195 cases: hand-written fixtures, scripted scenarios, and real patients from a research dataset. Hover any dot.

    Six language models answered them. Sonnet's claims were flagged least often; a small model, more than ten times as often. Switch the measure above the chart.

    Then the verifier itself was checked. People agreed with 18 of its 54 flags, so its rates are upper bounds: it errs on the side of suspicion.
